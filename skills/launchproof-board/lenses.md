# The five lenses

Summaries of published frameworks, in our own words. A board member applies ONE
lens and never speaks as the person the framework comes from, never quotes them
and never claims they would endorse anything.

| lens | source | the question it asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offers | Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* | Is this an offer people would feel foolish turning down? |
| Monopoly | Peter Thiel, *Zero to One* | Can this own a small market and keep it? |
| Demand | Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed*, *Key Person of Influence*, *24 Assets* | Can demand be built before the business is built? |
| Leverage | *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* (Eric Jorgenson) | Does this compound without the founder's hours? |
| Inversion | *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (Charlie Munger, ed. Peter Kaufman) | How does this fail, and is that avoidable? |

## The Offers lens

Source: Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (2021).

**Pick the market first.** The best offer fails in a bad market. Score the market
on four things: the pain is massive, the buyers have money to spend, they are
easy to find and reach, and the market is growing. A weak market is the most
common reason this lens passes.

**The value equation.** How much a buyer values an offer goes up with the dream
outcome and the perceived likelihood of getting it, and down with the time it
takes and the effort and sacrifice it asks. Score each of the four for this
business. Most offers fail on likelihood (no proof) or effort (too much asked of
the buyer).

**Do not compete on price.** If the only difference is being cheaper, it is a
commodity. Ask what would make this so good that people feel stupid saying no:
more of the outcome, faster, with less effort, with a guarantee.

**Build the stack.** List every problem the buyer hits on the way to the outcome,
turn each into a solution, keep the ones that are cheap to deliver and valuable
to the buyer, and stack them. Then strengthen it with real scarcity or urgency
(never fake), bonuses, a guarantee and a clear name.

Questions this lens asks: Who exactly has this pain and money? Why would they
believe it works? How fast do they get a result? What is the guarantee? Could you
double the price and still be the obvious choice?

## The Monopoly lens

Source: Peter Thiel with Blake Masters, *Zero to One* (2014).

**Competition destroys profit.** A business in a crowded market fights for thin
margins. The goal is a small market you can dominate, then expand from.

**What makes a durable business:** proprietary technology or know-how roughly ten
times better than the next option on something that matters; network effects;
economies of scale; a brand. Most small businesses have none of these, so ask
which one this could build.

**The seven questions:** the engineering question (is it a breakthrough, or an
incremental improvement?), timing (why now?), monopoly (does it start with a big
share of a small market?), people (is this the right team?), distribution (is
there a way to reach buyers, not just a product?), durability (will it still hold
its position in ten years?), and the secret (what does the founder know that
others do not?).

**Distribution is half of it.** A good product with no way to reach customers is
not a business. Ask how the first 100 customers arrive and what each one costs.

Questions this lens asks: Who else sells this within reach of the same buyer?
What is the small market this can own first? What is the secret? What stops a
copycat in month six?

## The Demand lens

Sources: Daniel Priestley, *Oversubscribed* (2015), *Key Person of Influence*
(2010, revised 2014) and *24 Assets* (2017).

**Demand first, supply second.** A business is strongest when more people want
in than it can serve. Before building, test whether the founder can collect
real signals of interest (a waitlist, deposits, people asking when it opens) and
whether demand could outrun capacity. A business that has to chase every buyer
is fighting uphill.

**Run it as a campaign, not a shop that is always open.** Demand builds in
cycles: share ideas and value openly, gather the people who raise their hands,
then open for a limited window with a real capacity. Ask whether this business
has a natural capacity limit and a launch rhythm, or whether it will be on
"permanent sale".

**The founder as a key person in a niche.** Buyers trust people they recognize
in a small, specific field. Check five things: can the founder state the pitch
in a few sentences, have they published (or could they publish) ideas that earn
trust, is there a product that captures what they know, do they have a profile
the niche recognizes, and which partnerships could lend them reach. Weakness
here usually means expensive, cold marketing.

**A product ecosystem, not a single product.** A free or low-cost entry point
that helps buyers self-qualify, a core offer, and a premium offer for the buyers
who want more. Ask what the entry point is and how a buyer moves up.

**Build assets, not just income.** A business that sells hours stops when the
founder stops. Look for assets that keep producing value: intellectual property,
a brand, a customer list, systems, content, a community, data. Ask which assets
the first year will create and which the founder already owns.

Questions this lens asks: Who is already asking for this? How many would join a
waitlist this month? What is the natural capacity limit? What is the entry
product? Which assets will exist in a year that do not exist today?

## The Leverage lens

Source: *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* by Eric Jorgenson (2020), a collection
of Naval Ravikant's public writing and talks.

**Specific knowledge.** The founder's edge should be knowledge that cannot easily
be trained into someone else: it comes from genuine curiosity, an unusual path or
a rare combination of skills. Ask whether this business runs on the founder's
specific knowledge, or on skills anyone could be hired to do.

**Leverage.** Output multiplies through people, capital, or products with no
marginal cost of copying (code and media). The last kind needs nobody's
permission. Ask which lever this business uses, and whether revenue can grow
without the founder's hours or headcount growing at the same rate. A business
that is purely the founder's time is a job.

**Own equity, earn while you sleep.** Wealth comes from owning assets that keep
earning, not from renting out time. Ask what the founder will own, and whether
the business still earns when the founder is not working.

**Accountability and judgment.** Putting your own name and reputation on the
line earns trust and leverage. Ask whether the founder is willing to be the face
of it, and whether the key decisions need good judgment the founder has.

**Play long-term games with long-term people.** Returns compound in
relationships, reputation and skill. Ask whether this is something the founder
would happily work on for ten years, with customers and partners who would stay
for ten years. A business chased only for money tends to be abandoned when it
gets hard.

Questions this lens asks: What does the founder know that is hard to copy? What
is the lever: code, media, capital or people? What does a month look like when
the founder takes it off? Is this a game the founder wants to play for a decade?

## The Inversion lens

Source: *Poor Charlie's Almanack: The Wit and Wisdom of Charles T. Munger*,
edited by Peter D. Kaufman (2005), and Munger's public talks.

**Invert.** Instead of asking how this succeeds, ask how it is most likely to
fail, then check whether each failure is avoidable. Avoiding a few big mistakes
beats finding one brilliant move. This lens always lists the failure modes
first.

**Circle of competence.** Is the founder operating inside what they genuinely
understand, and do they know where the edge of that circle is? Outside it, ask
what it would cost to learn or to bring in someone who knows.

**Incentives.** Look at what every party is rewarded for: customers, suppliers,
partners, staff and the founder. Plans that need people to act against their
own incentives fail. Ask who gains if this works and who loses.

**A durable moat and pricing power.** A good business can raise prices without
losing its customers, because something protects it: brand, switching costs,
cost advantage, or a local position nobody else can take. Ask what the moat is
and how it widens over time.

**Margin of safety.** Plans should survive being wrong. Ask what happens if sales
are half the forecast and costs are a third higher, and whether the founder can
still pay their bills. If one assumption has to go right, it is a bet, not a
plan.

**Psychology of misjudgment.** Founders misjudge in predictable ways: too much
optimism, seeing only confirming evidence, sunk costs, falling for what they
built, and copying the crowd. Name which of these is most likely at work in this
pitch.

**Opportunity cost.** Compare this with the founder's best alternative use of the
same money and years. A good idea that is worse than the founder's next best
option is still a pass.

Questions this lens asks: What are the three most likely ways this dies? Is the
founder inside their circle of competence? Whose incentives work against it?
What happens at half the sales? What is the best alternative use of this money
and time?
